Claude is useful for developer-led marketing work: writing first drafts fast, adapting tone, generating variants, and filling in structured content formats. The output needs editing — it won't know your voice or your users as well as you do — but it eliminates the blank-page problem and handles the structural work.

## Landing page copy

The key to getting usable landing page copy: give Claude the ICP and the differentiation, not just "write copy for my app."

```text
Write landing page copy for HOKM Online — a multiplayer platform
for Persian card games (HOKM, PASUR, Shelem, 7-e-Khabis).

Audience: Persian-speaking expats in North America and Europe,
25–45, who grew up playing these games and want to play with
family/friends across time zones. They know the games fluently.

Differentiation:
- The only platform that does all 4 major Persian card games
- Real-time with bots that sub in when players disconnect
- No account required to play (just share a link)

Sections needed:
- Hero: headline + subhead (not generic "Play anywhere, anytime")
- Problem: 2–3 sentences on the specific pain
- Feature highlights: 3 cards, concrete and specific
- Social proof placeholder
- CTA

Voice: direct, warm, assumes the reader knows these games.
Don't explain what HOKM is.
```

The "don't explain what HOKM is" instruction tells Claude the audience already has the context — a common oversight that produces patronizing copy.

## SEO content drafts

Claude is fast at drafting structured SEO content given a keyword and brief:

```text
Write a 600-word article targeting the query "play HOKM online with
friends." Structure:

- H1 matching the query
- Short intro (2 sentences)
- H2: How online HOKM works (brief, practical)
- H2: What to look for in an online HOKM platform
- H2: Playing HOKM across time zones (the specific use case)
- CTA pointing to [platform URL]

Facts to work in: supports 4 players, real-time, bot substitution
for disconnected players. Don't invent features.
```

Review and edit before publishing — Claude will sometimes add hedges ("may offer," "might consider") that kill SEO content, or make claims you haven't verified.

## Launch posts

For Product Hunt launches, social posts, or newsletter announcements, give Claude the format constraints and let it draft:

```text
Write a Product Hunt launch post for HOKM Online.

Tagline: [your tagline]
First paragraph: what it is, who it's for, one specific use case
Second paragraph: what makes it different from generic game platforms
Third paragraph: why I built this (brief, authentic)
CTA: Try it / upvote

Keep it under 250 words. First-person. No hype words
("revolutionary," "game-changing," "ultimate"). Don't use emoji.
```

Generate 2–3 variants and pick the best parts from each.

## Tone calibration

If Claude's default tone doesn't match your voice, calibrate it early:

```text
Here's an example of my writing: [paste a paragraph you've written]

Write [the thing] in this voice — same directness, similar sentence length.
```

Giving Claude an example of your own writing produces more accurate imitation than describing the voice abstractly ("professional but casual").

## What Claude is bad at for marketing

- **Knowing your actual users** — it writes for a generic version of your ICP, not the specific people you've talked to
- **Generating hooks** — it can write competent copy but struggles with the genuinely surprising angle; those usually come from a real customer quote or unexpected insight
- **Final polish** — Claude's marketing writing is competent but predictable; it needs a human editor to find the specific, unexpected detail that makes copy memorable

<details>
<summary>Common pitfall: publishing first-draft Claude copy without editing</summary>

Claude marketing copy is recognizable: parallel structure, balanced hedges, generic differentiators. "The only platform that..." followed by three bullet points, each starting with a verb. It reads fine; it doesn't stand out.

Use Claude for structure and coverage. Edit for voice, specificity, and the single unexpected line that makes someone remember the piece. That line almost always comes from a real customer conversation or your own unexpected observation about the product.
</details>
